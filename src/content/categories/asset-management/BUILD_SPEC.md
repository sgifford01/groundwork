## 1. Purpose and career fit

Returns are promised at acquisition and earned afterwards. Asset management is the owner's seat: it holds the business plan for each property and answers for its results. Portfolio management is one level up: which properties, markets and risks the fund should hold. Property management is one level down: running the building day to day. New analysts in asset management build budgets, explain variances, model capital projects and prepare investor reports; those who understand the portfolio level are the ones who get promoted. The three roles are confused constantly, so this category makes the difference explicit in every subtopic.

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Given a decision or task, the student can assign it to the property manager, asset manager or portfolio manager and justify it. (`asset-management-roles`)
2. Given a budget and actuals, the student can compute dollar and percentage variances with a consistent sign convention, label them favourable or unfavourable, identify the lines that matter and reforecast the year. (`asset-management-budgets`)
3. Given a capital project, the student can compute return on cost, payback and value created, and distinguish maintenance capex from value-add capex. (`asset-management-capex`)
4. Given renewal and new-lease terms, the student can compare the options and manage rollover exposure. (`asset-management-leasing`)
5. Given hold and sale projections, the student can compute the return on continuing to hold, compare it with selling or refinancing, and avoid sunk-cost reasoning. (`asset-management-hold-sell`)
6. Given property results, the student can assemble the core of an investor report and reconcile its figures. (`asset-management-reporting`)
7. Given a portfolio, the student can classify its strategy (core, core-plus, value-add, opportunistic), measure concentration and explain what fund leverage does to returns and risk. (`asset-management-portfolio`)
8. Given gross returns, fees and a promote, the student can compute net returns, distinguish fund from asset returns, and explain what a benchmark such as NCREIF's indices or ODCE measures. (`asset-management-fund-returns`)

## 4. Key concepts, definitions and formulas

- **Variance** (positive = favourable): revenue actual - budget; expense budget - actual. **Variance %** = variance / budget.
- **Reforecast** = year-to-date actual + revised remaining periods.
- **Return on cost** (capex) = incremental annual NOI / project cost. **Value created** = incremental NOI / cap rate.
- **Hold IRR:** the IRR of (minus today's net sale proceeds, future cash flows, future net sale proceeds).
- **Same-store growth:** growth of properties held in both periods.
- **Levered return** = (unlevered return - LTV x cost of debt) / (1 - LTV).
- **Net return** = gross return less fees and promote.
- **Strategies:** core (stabilised, low leverage, income-driven), core-plus (some leasing or light improvement), value-add (renovation or re-leasing, moderate to high leverage), opportunistic (development, distress; highest risk and target return). Describe by characteristics; do not assert numeric return bands as industry facts (give illustrative ranges, labelled).

#### Example 1: Budget vs. actual variance, favourable or unfavourable (`am-ex-variance`)

Sign convention: positive = favourable. Revenue variance = actual - budget. Expense variance = budget - actual. Variance % is measured against budget. NOI variance = revenue variance + expense variance.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Revenue, budget | `rev_budget` | $1,326,000 |
| Revenue, actual | `rev_actual` | $1,291,500 |
| Operating expenses, budget | `exp_budget` | $540,000 |
| Operating expenses, actual | `exp_actual` | $571,900 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Revenue variance | `rev_var` | `=rev_actual-rev_budget` | ($34,500) |
| Expense variance | `exp_var` | `=exp_budget-exp_actual` | ($31,900) |
| NOI variance | `noi_var` | `=rev_var+exp_var` | ($66,400) |
| NOI variance % of budget NOI | `noi_var_pct` | `=noi_var/(rev_budget-exp_budget)` | (8.4%) |
| Check: actual NOI - budget NOI | `check` | `=(rev_actual-exp_actual)-(rev_budget-exp_budget)` | ($66,400) |

*Note.* Both variances are unfavourable (negative). The check line equals the NOI variance, as it must.

#### Example 2: Mid-year reforecast (`am-ex-reforecast`)

A reforecast replaces the months that have happened with actuals and updates the view of the rest. Full-year forecast = year-to-date actual + remaining months at the revised run rate.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Full-year budget NOI | `budget_noi` | $1,572,000 |
| NOI, January to June actual | `ytd_actual` | $719,600 |
| Revised monthly NOI for July to December | `run_rate` | $127,000 |
| Months remaining | `months_left` | 6 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Full-year reforecast | `forecast` | `=ytd_actual+months_left*run_rate` | $1,481,600 |
| Reforecast vs. budget | `gap` | `=forecast-budget_noi` | ($90,400) |
| As % of budget | `gap_pct` | `=gap/budget_noi` | (5.8%) |

| Occupied units | `occupied` | 171 |

| Economic occupancy | `economic` | `=collected/potential` | 89.2% |

#### Example 4: Return on a unit renovation (`am-ex-capex-roi`)

Return on cost = annual rent premium / cost per unit. Value created = premium / cap rate. A project is worth doing if the value created comfortably exceeds its cost within the hold period.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Renovation cost per unit | `cost` | $12,000 |
| Rent premium per month | `premium` | $175 |
| Months vacant during the work | `downtime_months` | 1 |
| Rent before renovation | `old_rent` | $2,500 |
| Cap rate | `cap` | 5.25% |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Annual premium | `annual_premium` | `=premium*12` | $2,100 |
| All-in cost including lost rent | `all_in` | `=cost+downtime_months*old_rent` | $14,500 |
| Return on all-in cost | `roc` | `=annual_premium/all_in` | 14.5% |
| Payback (years) | `payback` | `=all_in/annual_premium` | 6.9 |
| Value created per unit | `value_created` | `=annual_premium/cap` | $40,000 |
| Value created less cost | `profit` | `=value_created-all_in` | $25,500 |

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Renewal rent per month | `renew_rent` | $2,700 |

| Months vacant if the tenant leaves | `vacant_months` | 1.5 |
| Turn cost (paint, cleaning, repairs) | `turn_cost` | $1,400 |
| Concession on the new lease (months) | `free_months` | 0.5 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| First-year income if renewed | `renew_income` | `=renew_rent*12` | $32,400 |

#### Example 6: Hold or sell: the return on staying in (`am-ex-hold-sell`)

The equity at stake in a hold decision is what you would net by selling TODAY, not what was invested originally. The IRR of holding is the return on that forgone cash from future cash flows and a later sale. Sell if it is below what the money could earn elsewhere at similar risk.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Net sale proceeds to equity today | `net_today` | $14,200,000 |
| Cash flow, year 1 of holding | `cf_a` | $760,000 |
| Cash flow, year 2 | `cf_b` | $795,000 |
| Cash flow, year 3 | `cf_c` | $830,000 |
| Net sale proceeds in year 3 | `net_later` | $15,900,000 |
| Return available on new deals | `hurdle` | 11% |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Year 3 cash flow plus sale | `year_three_total` | `=cf_c+net_later` | $16,730,000 |
| IRR of holding three more years | `hold_irr` | `=IRR({-net_today,cf_a,cf_b,year_three_total})` | 9.2% |
| Multiple on today’s equity | `multiple` | `=(cf_a+cf_b+year_three_total)/net_today` | 1.29x |
| Hold IRR minus the hurdle | `spread` | `=hold_irr-hurdle` | (1.8%) |

*Note.* Using the ORIGINAL equity instead of today’s net proceeds is the classic error (sunk cost). A negative spread argues for selling; a positive one for holding.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Unlevered return | `unlevered` | 7.0% |
| Fund LTV | `ltv` | 40% |
| Cost of debt | `debt_cost` | 5.5% |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Levered return | `levered` | `=(unlevered-ltv*debt_cost)/(1-ltv)` | 8.0% |

Investors receive the net return: the gross return less the management fee and the manager’s share of profit above a preferred return. This one-year version shows the mechanics; real funds compute it over the fund life.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Investor capital | `capital` | $100,000,000 |
| Gross return on capital | `gross` | 13.0% |
| Management fee (% of capital) | `fee` | 1.5% |
| Preferred return | `pref` | 8% |
| Manager’s share above the pref | `promote` | 20% |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Gross profit | `gross_profit` | `=capital*gross` | $13,000,000 |
| Management fee | `fee_amount` | `=capital*fee` | $1,500,000 |
| Profit after the fee | `after_fee` | `=gross_profit-fee_amount` | $11,500,000 |
| Promote | `promote_amount` | `=MAX(0,after_fee-capital*pref)*promote` | $700,000 |
| Net return to investors | `net` | `=(after_fee-promote_amount)/capital` | 10.8% |

## 5. Common misconceptions

1. Asset management is property management with a better title → different seat, different decisions.
2. The portfolio manager approves a unit renovation → that is asset-level; the portfolio level sets strategy and allocation.
3. An expense over budget is a positive variance → with positive = favourable it is negative.
4. Variance % is measured against actual → against budget.
5. NOI variance = revenue variance - expense variance when both are already signed → it is their sum.
6. Every variance needs action → separate timing differences from permanent ones and focus on material lines.
7. Capex reduces NOI → it is below the line (but it reduces cash flow).
8. A higher new-lease rent always beats a renewal → count downtime, turn cost and concessions.
9. Physical occupancy tells you revenue → economic occupancy does.
10. Hold/sell uses the original equity or the purchase price → it uses today's net proceeds (sunk cost).
11. "We can't sell at a loss" → the loss has already happened; the question is the best use of today's value.
12. A cash-out refinance is profit → it is borrowed money; risk rises.
13. Total NOI growth shows operating performance → use same-store.
14. Leverage raises returns → it magnifies both directions.
15. Gross return is what investors earn → net is.
16. A benchmark is a target return → it is a measure of what comparable portfolios earned.
17. Diversified means many properties → twenty apartment buildings in one submarket are not diversified.

## 8. Error hunts and spot-the-problem scenarios

1. Variance report with expense variances signed the wrong way and percentages on actual.
2. Reforecast that adds the full-year budget to year-to-date actuals (double count).
3. Capex analysis that treats the rent premium as monthly when computing return on cost and ignores downtime.
4. Renew-vs-re-lease sheet that omits vacancy and applies the concession to the renewal.
5. Hold/sell analysis that uses original equity as the opening outflow and gross sale price as proceeds.
6. Docreview: variance commentary ("expenses well controlled") contradicted by the numbers; a variance called favourable that is not.
7. Docreview: quarterly report page where occupancy quoted is physical while the text discusses revenue, same-store growth includes an acquisition, and distributions exceed cash flow without explanation.
8. Docreview: a fund fact sheet excerpt mixing gross and net returns and describing a concentrated portfolio as diversified.

## 9. Case study brief and rubric

**`asset-management-case-...` (type `timed`, level 3, about 40 minutes, pass mark 80%).** "A year in the life of one asset inside a fund."

*Brief.* A 180-unit apartment community (illustrative) is in year 3 of a value-add plan. Second sheet: budget and actuals for the year, a renovation log and a broker's opinion of value. Deliverables on the first sheet:

1. Variance report by line with correct signs and percentages; NOI variance.
2. Reforecast next year's NOI from the current run rate and remaining renovations.
3. Return on cost and value created for the remaining renovation units.
4. Net sale proceeds today, the hold IRR for two more years, and the spread to the fund's hurdle.
5. The fund-level effect: the asset's share of fund value and the levered return at the fund's LTV.

*Rubric (auto-graded; share of target cells).* Variance 25%, reforecast 15%, capex 20%, hold/sell 25%, fund level 15%. `mistakes` must cover: expense sign, variance on actual, monthly premium not annualised, original equity in the hold IRR, leverage formula without dividing by (1 - LTV).
