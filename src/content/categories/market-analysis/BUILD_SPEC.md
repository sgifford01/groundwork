## 1. Purpose and career fit

Every pro forma starts with a rent, a vacancy rate, a growth rate, an absorption pace and an exit cap. Interviewers and investment committees ask the same question about each: where did that come from? Market analysis is the craft of answering it. An intern is typically asked to pull comps, build an adjustment grid, summarise a submarket and flag the pipeline. This category teaches the measures, the comp method, the demand logic, the data sources and their limits, and how to turn research into assumptions with a stated confidence.

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Given inventory, occupancy, deliveries and absorption, the student can compute vacancy rates and net absorption and explain how they move together. (`market-analysis-supply-demand`)
2. Given employment, population and household data, the student can estimate demand for housing or space through an explicit chain of assumptions. (`market-analysis-drivers`)
3. Given a research question, the student can choose the right source (CoStar, census / ACS, BLS, broker reports, public records, shopping the comps) and state its main limitation. (`market-analysis-data`)
4. Given a subject and comparable rentals, the student can select comps, convert to effective rent, build an adjustment grid and conclude a rent. (`market-analysis-rent-comps`)
5. Given comparable sales, the student can compute price per unit, per SF and cap rate, adjust for differences and reconcile a value. (`market-analysis-sale-comps`)
6. Given a pipeline and absorption history, the student can estimate months of supply, a lease-up period and a stabilisation date. (`market-analysis-absorption`)
7. Given market research, the student can set rent, vacancy, growth and exit cap assumptions, and state the evidence and the downside case for each. (`market-analysis-assumptions`)

## 4. Key concepts, definitions and formulas

- **Inventory** (stock) changes with deliveries and removals. **Occupied stock** changes with net absorption. **Vacancy** = 1 - occupied / inventory.
- **Net absorption** is the change in occupied stock. Gross absorption (all leases signed) overstates demand.
- **Effective rent** is rent after concessions.
- **Adjustment grid:** comps are adjusted toward the subject, one feature at a time.
- **Capture rate / penetration rate:** how much of qualified demand a project, or a project plus its competitors, must win.
- **Stabilised vacancy:** the vacancy at which a market is in balance; used as the target for lease-up.
- **Trend vs. forecast:** past growth is evidence, not a prediction.

#### Example 1: Vacancy rate and net absorption (`mkt-ex-vacancy-absorption`)

Vacancy rate = vacant units / inventory. Net absorption = change in OCCUPIED units over a period. Deliveries add to inventory. Vacancy can rise even when absorption is positive, if deliveries are larger.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Inventory at start (units) | `inventory_start` | 8,000 |
| Occupied at start | `occupied_start` | 7,480 |
| Units delivered in the period | `deliveries` | 300 |
| Occupied at end | `occupied_end` | 7,640 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Vacancy at start | `vacancy_start` | `=(inventory_start-occupied_start)/inventory_start` | 6.5% |
| Net absorption (units) | `net_absorption` | `=occupied_end-occupied_start` | 160 |
| Inventory at end | `inventory_end` | `=inventory_start+deliveries` | 8,300 |
| Vacancy at end | `vacancy_end` | `=(inventory_end-occupied_end)/inventory_end` | 8.0% |

*Note.* Absorption of 160 units against 300 delivered: demand grew, and vacancy still rose.

#### Example 2: Months to return to stabilised vacancy (`mkt-ex-months-of-supply`)

Excess vacant units = vacant + pipeline - stabilised vacancy x (inventory + pipeline). Months = excess / monthly net absorption. A market is never absorbed to zero vacancy.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Existing inventory (units) | `inventory` | 8,000 |
| Vacant units | `vacant` | 560 |
| Under construction (units) | `pipeline` | 450 |
| Stabilised vacancy | `stab` | 5% |
| Net absorption per month | `absorb` | 40 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Vacant after delivery | `vacant_after` | `=vacant+pipeline` | 1,010 |
| Vacant units at stabilised vacancy | `stab_units` | `=stab*(inventory+pipeline)` | 422.5 |
| Excess to absorb | `excess` | `=vacant_after-stab_units` | 587.5 |
| Months to stabilise | `months` | `=excess/absorb` | 14.7 |

#### Example 3: Asking rent vs. effective rent (`mkt-ex-effective-rent`)

Concessions lower the rent actually collected. Effective rent = asking rent x paid months / lease months. Comps must be compared on effective rent.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Asking rent per month | `asking` | $2,800 |
| Lease term (months) | `lease_months` | 13 |
| Free months | `free_months` | 1 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Effective rent per month | `effective` | `=asking*(lease_months-free_months)/lease_months` | $2,585 |
| Concession as a % of asking | `discount` | `=1-effective/asking` | 7.7% |

#### Example 4: Adjusting one rent comp to the subject (`mkt-ex-rent-comp`)

Adjust the COMP toward the SUBJECT: adjustment = (subject - comp) x value per unit of the feature. Inferior comp → adjust up. Superior comp → adjust down.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Comp effective rent | `comp_rent` | $2,650 |
| Subject size (SF) | `subject_sf` | 720 |
| Comp size (SF) | `comp_sf` | 760 |
| Value of size ($/SF/month) | `sf_value` | $1.50 |
| Subject year built | `subject_year` | 2026 |
| Comp year built | `comp_year` | 2019 |
| Value of each year newer ($/month) | `year_value` | $15 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Size adjustment | `size_adj` | `=(subject_sf-comp_sf)*sf_value` | ($60) |
| Age adjustment | `age_adj` | `=(subject_year-comp_year)*year_value` | $105 |
| Adjusted comp rent | `adjusted` | `=comp_rent+size_adj+age_adj` | $2,695 |
| Net adjustment as % of comp rent | `net_adj` | `=(adjusted-comp_rent)/comp_rent` | 1.7% |

*Note.* A comp needing large net or gross adjustments is a weak comp. Many practitioners distrust a comp once gross adjustments pass roughly a quarter of its rent; treat that as a rule of thumb, not a standard.

#### Example 5: From job growth to apartment demand (`mkt-ex-jobs-to-demand`)

A simple demand chain: new jobs → new households (jobs / jobs per household) → renter households (x renter share) → demand the subject can capture (x capture rate). Every link is an assumption to source and defend.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| New jobs per year in the market area | `new_jobs` | 6,000 |
| Jobs per household | `jobs_per_hh` | 1.40 |
| Share of households that rent | `renter_share` | 48% |
| Capture rate for the subject | `capture` | 3% |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| New households | `households` | `=new_jobs/jobs_per_hh` | 4,286 |
| New renter households | `renters` | `=households*renter_share` | 2,057 |
| Units the subject can lease per year | `subject_demand` | `=renters*capture` | 61.7 |

#### Example 6: Income needed to afford a rent (`mkt-ex-affordability`)

A common screen is that rent should not exceed a stated share of gross income (30% is the usual convention). Required income = annual rent / share.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Rent per month | `rent` | $2,800 |
| Maximum share of income on rent | `share` | 30% |
| Area median household income (illustrative) | `median_income` | $105,000 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Income required | `required_income` | `=rent*12/share` | $112,000 |
| Required income as % of the median | `vs_median` | `=required_income/median_income` | 107% |
| Rent the median household can afford | `affordable_rent` | `=median_income*share/12` | $2,625 |

#### Example 7: Capture and penetration rates (`mkt-ex-capture-rate`)

Capture rate = the subject’s units / qualified demand. Penetration rate = (subject + competing units) / qualified demand. High rates mean the project needs an unrealistic share of the market.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Subject units | `subject_units` | 250 |
| Competing new units | `competing_units` | 900 |
| Income-qualified renter households in the area | `qualified` | 9,500 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Capture rate | `capture` | `=subject_units/qualified` | 2.6% |
| Penetration rate | `penetration` | `=(subject_units+competing_units)/qualified` | 12.1% |

#### Example 8: Sale comp metrics (`mkt-ex-sale-comp`)

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Sale price | `price` | $42,000,000 |
| Units | `units` | 120 |
| Net rentable area (SF) | `net_sf` | 102,000 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|

#### Example 9: Reconciling a value from sale comps (`mkt-ex-value-from-comps`)

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Subject NOI | `subject_noi` | $1,800,000 |
| Concluded cap rate | `cap` | 5.25% |
| Subject units | `subject_units` | 96 |
| Concluded price per unit | `comp_per_unit` | $345,000 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Value by cap rate | `by_cap` | `=subject_noi/cap` | $34,285,714 |

#### Example 10: Lease-up period for a new building (`mkt-ex-lease-up`)

Months to stabilise = (units at stabilised occupancy - pre-leased units) / units leased per month. Pace comes from comparable lease-ups, not from hope.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Units | `units` | 250 |
| Stabilised occupancy | `stab_occ` | 95% |
| Pre-leased at opening | `preleased` | 30 |
| Net leases per month | `pace` | 18 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Occupied units at stabilisation | `stab_units` | `=units*stab_occ` | 237.5 |
| Units still to lease | `to_lease` | `=stab_units-preleased` | 207.5 |
| Months to stabilise | `months` | `=to_lease/pace` | 11.5 |

#### Example 11: Compound annual rent growth (`mkt-ex-rent-growth`)

CAGR = (end / start)^(1 / years) - 1. Never divide the total change by the number of years.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Average rent five years ago | `rent_then` | $2,150 |
| Average rent now | `rent_now` | $2,650 |
| Years | `years` | 5 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Compound annual growth | `cagr` | `=(rent_now/rent_then)^(1/years)-1` | 4.27% |
| TRAP: total change / years | `simple` | `=(rent_now/rent_then-1)/years` | 4.65% |
| Rent in 3 years at the CAGR | `projected` | `=rent_now*(1+cagr)^3` | $3,004 |

Two quick gauges of supply risk: pipeline as a share of inventory, and years of supply = pipeline / annual net absorption.

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Pipeline as % of inventory | `pipeline_share` | `=pipeline/inventory` | 7.5% |
| Years of supply | `years_of_supply` | `=pipeline/annual_absorption` | 1.25 |

## 5. Common misconceptions

1. Absorption is the number of leases signed → net absorption is the change in occupied units.
2. Positive absorption means falling vacancy → not if deliveries exceed it.
3. Adjusting the subject to the comp → always adjust the comp to the subject.
4. A superior comp is adjusted up → down.
5. Comparing asking rents with effective rents.
6. Averaging comps without adjusting, or weighting a poor comp equally with a good one.
7. Rent per SF is constant across unit sizes → it falls as units get larger.
8. Absorbing to zero vacancy → markets stabilise at a positive vacancy.
9. Ignoring the pipeline, or counting proposed projects as if certain.
10. Stabilised vacancy applied to today's inventory rather than inventory after deliveries.
11. Growth rate = total change / years → use the compound rate.
12. Trailing growth will continue → a forecast needs a reason.
13. Cap rate from trailing NOI compared with one from forward NOI.
14. A 1-year ACS estimate for a small area is precise → small areas have wide margins of error; use 5-year data.
15. A broker report is neutral research → it is marketing with useful data in it.
16. Capture rate and penetration rate are the same thing.

## 8. Error hunts and spot-the-problem scenarios

1. Submarket table where absorption is the change in inventory and vacancy uses the prior year's inventory.
2. Adjustment grid with one comp adjusted in the wrong direction and the conclusion averaging raw rents.
3. Sale comp table mixing trailing and forward NOI, with price per SF divided by gross instead of net area.
4. Lease-up schedule that passes 100% occupancy and ignores pre-leasing.
5. Docreview: a fictional broker report claiming "record absorption" from gross leasing, quoting asking rents as growth evidence, and omitting the pipeline.
6. Docreview: a market summary using a 1-year survey estimate for a small area as if exact, and a trailing growth rate as a forecast.

## 9. Case study brief and rubric

**`market-analysis-case-...` (type `timed`, level 3, about 40 minutes, pass mark 80%).**

*Brief.* A developer is considering 220 apartments in an illustrative San Diego submarket. On a second sheet the student gets: a five-year submarket history, a pipeline list, five rent comps and three sale comps (all illustrative). Deliverables on the first sheet:

1. Current vacancy, average annual net absorption, rent CAGR.
2. Rent conclusion for the subject's one-bed from an adjustment grid.
3. Pipeline-adjusted months to stabilised vacancy and a lease-up period for the subject.
4. A going-in cap from the sale comps and a reconciled value indication.
5. The pro forma inputs: rent, vacancy, growth, lease-up months, exit cap, each a formula tied to the evidence.

*Rubric (auto-graded; share of target cells).* Market metrics 20%, rent conclusion 25%, absorption and lease-up 20%, sale comps 15%, assumption block 20%. `mistakes` must cover: absorption from inventory, wrong-direction adjustment, zero-vacancy absorption, mixed NOI bases, simple instead of compound growth.
