## 1. Purpose and career fit

Acquisitions is the most common entry-level seat in real estate investment. The work is: screen deals, underwrite from the seller's documents, help negotiate the letter of intent and purchase agreement, run due diligence, write the investment committee memo, and get to closing. Analysts are judged on whether their numbers survive contact with the documents. This category works from documents: operating statements, rent rolls, contract excerpts, title report excerpts and settlement figures. It uses multifamily for underwriting examples (commercial lease mechanics belong to `argus`).

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Given a deal source and an offering memo, the student can separate facts from marketing and list what needs independent support. (`acquisitions-sourcing`)
2. Given a T-12 and a rent roll, the student can normalise income and expenses to an underwritten NOI, compute loss-to-lease and economic occupancy, and price the deal. (`acquisitions-underwriting`)
3. Given LOI or PSA terms, the student can say what each term does, who bears a stated risk, and when the deposit goes hard. (`acquisitions-contracts`)
4. Given a due diligence finding, the student can classify it (physical, financial, legal, environmental), size it, and choose between proceeding, re-trading and walking. (`acquisitions-diligence`)
5. Given deal facts, the student can outline an IC memo: recommendation, thesis, returns, risks and mitigants. (`acquisitions-ic-memo`)
6. Given a preliminary title report excerpt, the student can tell routine exceptions from ones needing action, and explain what escrow and title insurance do. (`acquisitions-escrow-title`)
7. Given a closing date and property data, the student can compute prorations, closing costs, sources and uses, and the cash to wire. (`acquisitions-prorations`)
8. Given a contract's key dates, the student can build the deal calendar and explain how a commercial closing differs from a house purchase. (`acquisitions-timeline`)

## 4. Key concepts, definitions and formulas

- **T-12:** the trailing twelve months of actual income and expenses. **Underwritten NOI:** the buyer's expected first-year NOI.
- **Normalising:** removing non-recurring items, resetting what a sale changes, replacing owner-specific costs with market costs.
- **Loss-to-lease:** market rent minus in-place rent. **Economic occupancy:** collected rent / potential rent.
- **LOI:** a mostly non-binding summary of terms. **PSA:** the binding contract.
- **Due diligence period:** the buyer's window to investigate and cancel. **Going hard:** the deposit becomes non-refundable.
- **Escrow:** a neutral party holds documents and money and closes when both sides' instructions are met. **Title insurance:** insures against defects in title as of the policy date; **exceptions** are what it does not cover.
- **Proration:** splitting income and expenses between buyer and seller to the day.

## 5. Common misconceptions

1. The broker's pro forma NOI is the NOI to capitalise → underwrite your own.
2. The seller's property tax carries over → a sale usually resets the assessment (verify the rule and cite it).
3. A self-managed property has no management cost → underwrite a market fee.
4. One-time income is income → remove it.
5. Loss-to-lease is captured on day one → only as leases roll, and only if market rent holds.
6. Physical occupancy equals economic occupancy.
7. Reserves are optional because the T-12 shows none.
8. An LOI binds the parties to close → generally only specific clauses (confidentiality, exclusivity) are binding.
9. The deposit is always refundable → only until it goes hard.
10. Title insurance covers future problems → it covers defects existing at the policy date.
11. Every title exception is a problem → many are routine; the task is to tell which are not.
12. Security deposits belong to the seller → they belong to the tenants and transfer as a credit.
13. Credits to the buyer are added to the cash due → they reduce it.
14. The closing day belongs to the seller (or buyer) by default → the contract decides.
15. A price reduction and a closing credit are identical → a credit may be limited by the lender and changes the loan basis differently.
16. A commercial closing works like a house purchase → longer diligence, negotiated contract, entity buyers, lender requirements, estoppels.

## 8. Error hunts and spot-the-problem scenarios

1. Underwriting sheet that leaves the seller's tax bill in place and capitalises NOI including a one-time item.
2. Rent roll summary that divides loss-to-lease by in-place rent and counts a vacant unit's market rent as collected.
3. Proration sheet that gives the closing day to the wrong party and adds credits to cash due.
4. Settlement statement with the security deposits on the wrong side and the deposit not credited.
5. Tax proration that uses the buyer's days when the tax is unpaid.
6. Docreview (seed): seller's T-12.
7. Docreview: OM summary page ("pro forma" rents presented as in-place, cap rate on next year's NOI, "below replacement cost" with no support).
8. Docreview: PSA excerpt with a diligence period that ends after the deposit goes hard, a closing date before the financing contingency expires, and an "as-is" clause contradicting a repair obligation.
9. Docreview: preliminary title report excerpt: routine tax and utility easement exceptions plus an unreleased old deed of trust and a recorded access easement across the parking lot.
10. Docreview: IC memo excerpt whose sources do not equal uses and whose stated cap rate does not match price and NOI.

## 9. Case study brief and rubric

**`acquisitions-case-...` (type `timed`, level 3, about 45 minutes, pass mark 80%).**

*Brief.* A 72-unit apartment building in an illustrative San Diego neighbourhood is offered at a stated price. Second sheet: the seller's T-12 and a rent roll summary. The student delivers, on the first sheet:

1. Underwritten NOI with at least four normalising adjustments.
2. Loss-to-lease and economic occupancy.
3. An offer price at a target cap rate and its discount to asking.
4. Deposits and the amount at risk after diligence.
5. Prorations for a mid-month closing (rent, deposits, tax).
6. Sources and uses, and the cash to wire.

*Rubric (auto-graded; share of target cells).* Underwriting 35%, pricing 10%, deposits 10%, prorations 25%, sources and uses 20%. `mistakes` must cover: taxes not reset, one-time income kept, loss-to-lease on the wrong base, closing-day off-by-one, credits added instead of subtracted, deposit not credited.
