## 1. Purpose and career fit

Debt is usually most of the money in a deal and sets many of its rules: how much can be borrowed, what must be reported, what happens if the plan slips. Developers negotiate with lenders constantly, and many graduates start on the lending side. This category teaches loan products and terms from the lender's point of view, so that the student can predict what a lender will offer and why, read a term sheet, and understand the capital stack above and below the senior loan.

The core Excel course already teaches PMT, amortisation and the LTV / DSCR / debt yield formulas as spreadsheet skills. This category may reuse them (re-teaching the Excel in hints) but its subject is the lending decision and the products.

## 2. Learning objectives

1. Given a business plan (ground-up, value-add, stabilised hold), the student can match it to a loan type (construction, bridge, permanent, agency) and explain the fit. (`capital-markets-loan-types`)
2. Given term-sheet items (rate type, term, amortisation, interest-only period, extension options, fees, prepayment), the student can say what each means and compute its cost. (`capital-markets-loan-types`)
3. Given a capital stack, the student can compute each layer's share and attachment point, the blended cost, and each layer's loss for a given fall in value; and can distinguish mezzanine debt from preferred equity. (`capital-markets-stack`)
4. Given an index, spread, floor and cap, the student can compute the all-in rate, interest cost and cap payments. (`capital-markets-rates`)
5. Given NOI, value, cost and a lender's tests, the student can size a loan and name the binding constraint, for permanent and construction loans. (`capital-markets-sizing`)
6. Given loan terms, the student can say what the borrower and guarantor are liable for under non-recourse with carve-outs, completion guarantees and repayment guarantees. (`capital-markets-recourse`)
7. Given a stabilised or struggling property, the student can compute refinance proceeds, cash out or cash in, prepayment cost, and an extension-test paydown. (`capital-markets-refinance`)

## 4. Key concepts, definitions and formulas

- **LTV** = loan / value. **LTC** = loan / total cost. **DSCR** = NOI / annual debt service. **Debt yield** = NOI / loan.
- **Loan constant** = annual debt service / loan.
- **All-in floating rate** = MAX(index, floor) + spread. **Basis point** = 0.01%.
- **Cap payoff** = MAX(0, index - strike) x notional.
- **Attachment point:** the LTV at which a layer starts; **detachment point:** where it ends. A layer from 60% to 75% LTV loses money only if value falls more than 25%, and is wiped out at a 40% fall.
- **Recourse:** the lender can pursue the borrower's or guarantor's other assets. **Non-recourse:** the lender's remedy is the property, except for carve-outs.

#### Example 1: Capital stack shares and blended cost (`cap-ex-stack`)

The capital stack orders the money in a deal by priority: senior debt is repaid first and is cheapest; mezzanine and preferred equity sit in the middle; common equity is last and most expensive. Blended cost = sum of share x cost.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Senior loan share of cost | `senior_share` | 60% |
| Senior loan rate | `senior_cost` | 6.50% |
| Mezzanine share | `mezz_share` | 15% |
| Mezzanine rate | `mezz_cost` | 11.00% |
| Equity target return | `equity_cost` | 18.00% |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Common equity share | `equity_share` | `=1-senior_share-mezz_share` | 25% |
| Blended cost of capital | `blended` | `=senior_share*senior_cost+mezz_share*mezz_cost+equity_share*equity_cost` | 10.05% |
| Blended cost of the debt layers | `debt_only` | `=(senior_share*senior_cost+mezz_share*mezz_cost)/(senior_share+mezz_share)` | 7.40% |

#### Example 2: Who loses when value falls (`cap-ex-loss`)

Losses are absorbed from the bottom of the stack up. If value falls by more than the equity share, the next layer starts to lose. This is why junior capital costs more.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Original value | `value` | $50,000,000 |
| Senior loan | `senior` | $30,000,000 |
| Mezzanine loan | `mezz` | $7,500,000 |
| Fall in value | `decline` | 30% |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Common equity | `equity` | `=value-senior-mezz` | $12,500,000 |
| Value after the fall | `new_value` | `=value*(1-decline)` | $35,000,000 |
| Equity remaining | `equity_left` | `=MAX(0,new_value-senior-mezz)` | $0 |
| Mezzanine recovered | `mezz_recovery` | `=MAX(0,MIN(mezz,new_value-senior))` | $5,000,000 |
| Mezzanine loss | `mezz_loss` | `=1-mezz_recovery/mezz` | 33.3% |
| Senior recovered | `senior_recovery` | `=MIN(senior,new_value)` | $30,000,000 |

#### Example 3: Floating rate with an index floor (`cap-ex-floating`)

A floating-rate loan charges an index (SOFR) plus a spread. A floor sets a minimum for the index: all-in rate = MAX(index, floor) + spread. Spreads are quoted in basis points (100 bps = 1%).

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| SOFR (illustrative) | `sofr` | 4.30% |
| Spread (bps) | `spread_bps` | 300 |
| Index floor | `floor` | 3.00% |
| SOFR in a low-rate scenario | `low_sofr` | 2.00% |
| Loan balance | `loan` | $30,000,000 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| All-in rate today | `rate` | `=MAX(sofr,floor)+spread_bps/10000` | 7.30% |
| All-in rate if SOFR falls | `rate_low` | `=MAX(low_sofr,floor)+spread_bps/10000` | 6.00% |
| Annual interest today | `interest` | `=loan*rate` | $2,190,000 |

#### Example 4: Interest rate cap payoff (`cap-ex-cap`)

A cap pays the borrower (index - strike) x balance whenever the index is above the strike, and nothing otherwise. The loan itself is unchanged.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Loan balance | `loan` | $30,000,000 |
| Cap strike | `strike` | 4.50% |
| SOFR for the year | `sofr` | 5.40% |
| Spread | `spread` | 3.00% |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Interest paid to the lender | `lender_interest` | `=loan*(sofr+spread)` | $2,520,000 |
| Paid by the cap provider | `cap_payment` | `=MAX(0,sofr-strike)*loan` | $270,000 |
| Net interest cost | `net_cost` | `=lender_interest-cap_payment` | $2,250,000 |
| Effective rate | `effective_rate` | `=net_cost/loan` | 7.50% |

#### Example 5: Permanent loan sizing: the lender takes the lowest of three (`cap-ex-sizing`)

A lender sizes to several tests and lends the smallest result: loan-to-value (value x LTV), debt service coverage (the loan whose payment is NOI / DSCR) and debt yield (NOI / minimum debt yield).

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Underwritten NOI | `noi` | $2,400,000 |
| Appraised value | `value` | $40,000,000 |
| Maximum LTV | `max_ltv` | 65% |
| Minimum DSCR | `min_dscr` | 1.25 |
| Loan rate | `rate` | 6.50% |
| Amortisation (years) | `amort_years` | 30 |
| Minimum debt yield | `min_dy` | 9% |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Loan by LTV | `by_ltv` | `=value*max_ltv` | $26,000,000 |
| Largest annual debt service | `max_payment` | `=noi/min_dscr` | $1,920,000 |
| Loan by DSCR | `by_dscr` | `=PV(rate/12,amort_years*12,-max_payment/12)` | $25,313,731 |
| Loan by debt yield | `by_dy` | `=noi/min_dy` | $26,666,667 |
| Loan offered | `loan` | `=MIN(by_ltv,by_dscr,by_dy)` | $25,313,731 |

*Note.* DSCR binds here. If rates fell, the DSCR loan would grow and LTV would take over.

#### Example 9: Debt yield extension test and the paydown to pass it (`cap-ex-extension-test`)

Debt yield = NOI / loan. If an extension requires a minimum debt yield, the largest loan that passes is NOI / test, and the paydown is the excess.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Loan balance | `loan` | $30,000,000 |
| NOI | `noi` | $2,070,000 |
| Required debt yield | `test` | 8.0% |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Debt yield today | `dy` | `=noi/loan` | 6.9% |
| Largest loan that passes | `max_loan` | `=noi/test` | $25,875,000 |
| Paydown needed | `paydown` | `=MAX(0,loan-max_loan)` | $4,125,000 |
| Or: NOI needed with no paydown | `noi_needed` | `=loan*test` | $2,400,000 |

#### Example 10: Positive and negative leverage (`cap-ex-leverage`)

The loan constant is annual debt service / loan. If the property’s cap rate is above the loan constant, borrowing raises the cash-on-cash return (positive leverage); if below, it lowers it.

| Given | Name | Value |
|---|---|---:|
| Price | `price` | $40,000,000 |
| Cap rate | `cap` | 5.50% |
| LTV | `ltv` | 60% |
| Loan rate | `rate` | 6.50% |
| Amortisation (years) | `amort_years` | 30 |

| Step | Name | Formula | Result |
|---|---|---|---:|
| Loan | `loan` | `=price*ltv` | $24,000,000 |
| Annual debt service | `debt_service` | `=-PMT(rate/12,amort_years*12,loan)*12` | $1,820,356 |
| Loan constant | `constant` | `=debt_service/loan` | 7.58% |
| Cash-on-cash return | `cash_on_cash` | `=(price*cap-debt_service)/(price-loan)` | 2.37% |
| Cap rate minus loan constant | `spread` | `=cap-constant` | (2.08%) |

*Note.* The cap rate (5.50%) is below the loan constant, so leverage is negative: cash-on-cash is lower than the unlevered 5.50%.

Many loans charge a penalty for early repayment. A step-down schedule (for example 3%, 2%, 1% of the balance in years 1, 2, 3) is the simplest form. Yield maintenance and defeasance are the alternatives on fixed-rate loans: explain them in words only.

| Net proceeds to equity | `net_proceeds` | `=sale_price*(1-sale_costs)-balance-penalty` | $14,069,000 |

## 5. Common misconceptions

1. A cap limits the all-in rate → it limits the index; the spread is on top.
2. A cap lowers what the lender is paid → the lender is paid in full; a third party pays the borrower.
3. A floor protects the borrower → it protects the lender.
4. The lender lends the highest of its tests → the lowest.
5. DSCR uses cash flow after debt, or the loan amount → NOI / debt service.
6. Debt yield depends on the interest rate → it does not; that is why lenders use it.
7. LTV and LTC are interchangeable → value vs. cost; a construction loan is tested on both.
8. Mezzanine debt is secured by the property → by the ownership interests in the borrower.
9. Preferred equity is debt → it is equity with priority; remedies differ.
10. Non-recourse means no personal exposure at all → carve-outs and guarantees remain.
11. More leverage always raises returns → only while the cap rate exceeds the loan constant (positive leverage), and it always raises risk.
12. Refinance proceeds are all cash out → the old loan and costs come off first.
13. An interest-only loan is "cheaper" → lower payments, no principal reduction, same or higher balloon.
14. Extension options are automatic → they have tests and fees.
15. Losses are shared pro rata across the stack → they are absorbed bottom-up.

## 9. Case study brief and rubric

**`capital-markets-case-...` (type `case`, level 3, guided, about 15 minutes).** Finance one illustrative apartment development from construction to permanent loan.

*Steps:* (1) construction loan as the lesser of LTC and LTV on completed value, and the equity that goes in first; (2) floating-rate interest for two years with a floor and a cap; (3) stabilised value and permanent loan as the lowest of three tests; (4) payoff, costs and cash out or cash in; (5) the capital stack after refinancing, with a mezzanine layer, and the loss to each layer in a stated downturn.
